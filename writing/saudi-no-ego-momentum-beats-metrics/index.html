Saudi Arabia Admits What the UK Never Could: "We Have No Ego"

A Saudi finance minister said something I never heard in fifteen years in British government. It explains why the Gulf builds and we deliberate.

Phil Broadhead OBE 6 February 2026, Dubai. 3 min read.
Share X LinkedIn Saudi Arabia, delivery, government

In December 2025, Saudi Finance Minister Mohammed Al-Jadaan said something I've never heard from a UK minister in 15 years working at the highest levels of government:

"We have no ego - absolutely no ego. If we announce something and we need to adjust it, accelerate it, defer or cancel it, we will without blinking."

He was explaining why NEOM's "The Line" had been scaled from 170 kilometers to 2.4 kilometers. Why Red Sea hotels were opening sequentially instead of waiting for the full master plan. Why the 2034 World Cup was getting priority over open-ended developments.

Stop trying to get everyone to agree on what success looks like. Start making decisions that can't be unmade.

Saudi Arabia has learned what I watched the UK struggle with for years: momentum beats metrics.

Having worked on countless major regeneration projects and advised on transformation across government and corporate sectors, I've sat through an untold number of sessions where we got trapped in the same loop:

"What does success look like?".

Everyone adds their metric. The developer wants ROI. The Council wants affordable housing. The community wants green space. The Treasury wants jobs. The environmental groups want net zero.

Suddenly you're not building anything; you're building a performance framework.

I learned the hard way - working with the ever pragmatic Michael Gove at MHCLG - that the only question that matters is this:

"What's the next irreversible decision?".

Not the end state. Not the vision everyone agrees on. The next door you can't walk back through.

Should we acquire this land?

Should we sign this anchor tenant?

Should we break ground on Phase 1?

Make that decision. See what breaks. Adjust.

The GCC has systematised this in ways the UK never managed.

When Red Sea Global opened Six Senses Southern Dunes in November 2023, the first paying guests at any Saudi giga-project, they didn't wait for consensus on what the full resort destination would look like.

They made one irreversible decision: open the first hotel.

That created momentum for the next decision: open St. Regis in January 2024.

Then Nujuma Ritz-Carlton in May 2024 ($3,431/night - Middle East's most expensive hotel).

Then Shebara in November 2024.

By September 2025, they'd opened nine hotels and secured a $3.76 billion green loan - because investors don't fund visions, they fund momentum.

Six Flags Qiddiya opened December 31, 2025. (My family is very excited about this and we're off there in a few months!) It's three years late and slightly scaled back from original plans, but it opened. With paying customers. Operating roller coasters - including the fastest and longest in the world. And generating revenue.

Compare that to the UK approach I witnessed repeatedly: we'd spend 18 months getting stakeholder consensus on "what success looks like" for a regeneration project, only to find that by the time we had agreement, the market had shifted, the funding model had changed, or political priorities had moved on.

The breakthrough I saw at MHCLG was when we stopped asking "what's the perfect plan?" and started asking "what can we deliver in the next 12 months?".

Not "how do we solve housing nationally" - that creates analysis paralysis.

Instead: "Can we get planning permission for this specific site by Q2?"

That's an irreversible decision. You either get it or you don't. Then you make the next decision.

What I'm watching in the GCC now is this approach at scale.

Dubai's Palm Jebel Ali: awarded road infrastructure contracts in May 2024, marine works in August 2024, villa construction in October 2024. Each contract is irreversible. Each one unlocks the next phase.

They're not waiting for consensus on what the full Palm will be in 2040. They're building Frond 1. I'm watching it day by day and the momentum is palpable.

The Saudi "recalibration" - scaling The Line from 170km to 2.4km - isn't failure. It's pragmatism.

They made an irreversible decision: lock in the 2034 World Cup. That created a hard deadline. Now every other project gets prioritised or deferred based on "does this support 2034?".

That's how you escape planning mode.

For anyone leading transformation - in government, property, or corporate restructuring:

Stop trying to get everyone to agree on what success looks like.

Start making decisions that can't be unmade.

Acquire the land. Sign the anchor tenant. Award the first construction contract. Open the first phase.

Each irreversible decision teaches you something the business case never could.

Momentum beats metrics. Every time.
