"Sports City." "Academic City." "Production City." "Um Suqeim Beach Project."

Having worked on £3bn+ of UK regeneration, including founding and chairing one of the UK's largest Urban Regeneration Companies, I've seen every approach to naming major developments. Dubai's literal naming strategy initially seemed almost too simple.

It's actually a masterclass in strategic clarity.

Western regeneration typically does this:

Launch "Ocean Village" or "Royal Quays" - aspirational names that promise a lifestyle before laying a single brick. Can create false hope or even opposition because the vision is being sold before it's earned. Spend years in consultation because nobody knows what's actually being delivered.

I've sat through countless branding workshops where decision makers agonised over finding the "perfect" name that would capture a future we hadn't yet built.

Dubai does this:

Call it exactly what it is. "Production City" tells you it's for manufacturing and logistics. "Academic City" signals education clusters. "Internet City" attracted tech companies because the name itself was the pitch.

The function becomes the brand. The brand attracts the right investment. The investment delivers the function. It's a virtuous circle.

## What makes this approach work

1. Transparency beats aspiration

When a developer announces "Umm Suqeim Beach Project," everyone knows exactly what's being built, where, and why. No mystery. No overpromising. Just clear intent.

2. Names can drive zoning

By calling something "Media City" or "Healthcare City," Dubai isn't just describing it - they're programming it. The name tells businesses "this is for you" and tells others "probably not for you." It's urban planning through nomenclature.

3. Literal names scale

When you have dozens of major developments running simultaneously, "Sports City" and "Motor City" are easier to track, fund, and regulate than a portfolio of poetic names that all blur together.

## Looking back at the UK projects that actually worked

King's Cross - Kept the historical name, delivered the infrastructure, let the place identity emerge organically (Coal Drops Yard, Granary Square).

Canary Wharf - Geographic descriptor, nothing aspirational. The finance district reputation came from what was built, not what was promised.

Salford Quays - Literal location name. Became MediaCityUK only after the BBC actually arrived.

Stratford - Olympic development kept the area's existing name. Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park emerged later, after delivery.

The pattern is clear. During my time leading major regeneration programs across the UK, the developments that quietly delivered infrastructure first and let identity follow consistently outlasted the ones that led with aspirational branding.

Dubai has systematized what UK regeneration discovered through trial and error. Instead of accidentally stumbling into honest naming after expensive rebrands, they start with radical clarity about function and let success speak for itself.

There's a lesson here for anyone working in transformation: sometimes the smartest brand strategy is no brand strategy. Just radical honesty about what you're building and who it's for.
